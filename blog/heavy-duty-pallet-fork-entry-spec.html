Heavy Duty Pallet Fork Entry & Entry-Style Specification Guide

Heavydutypallets Editorial • November 2026

Why Fork Entry Style Drives Throughput

For procurement and logistics teams standardizing on heavy duty pallets, fork entry style is one of the most consequential specifications on the data sheet. It determines how many directions a forklift or pallet jack can engage the pallet, how quickly operators can pick and stage loads, and whether the unit integrates cleanly with automated conveyors and shuttle systems. Choosing the wrong entry style can quietly erode labor productivity across every shift, every day, for the life of the fleet.

The two dominant configurations are two-way and four-way entry. Two-way pallets accept fork tines from only two opposing faces, while four-way pallets accept entry from all four sides. Four-way designs cost modestly more but recover that premium through faster handling in congested docks and narrow aisles where repositioning a pallet to align the forks is impractical.

Two-Way vs Four-Way Entry

Two-way entry works well in operations with consistent load orientation, such as single-direction conveyor lines or rack bays always approached from the same aisle. The solid bottom deck common to two-way designs also improves support on flow rails and chain conveyors. However, the moment a load must be approached from an adjacent face, operators lose time maneuvering.

Four-way entry is the default recommendation for mixed-use distribution centers. Full four-way pallets provide forklift access on all sides and pallet-jack access on at least two, which matters for ground-level picking. When evaluating heavy duty PP pallets from Elipacko, confirm whether the jack-accessible faces match your most frequent handling direction, because partial four-way designs restrict pallet-jack entry to the fork-notch faces only.

Deck Configuration and Entry Height

Entry height, the vertical clearance between the top and bottom decks, must exceed the thickness of your heaviest fork tines with margin for worn or non-standard equipment. Most heavy duty plastic pallets provide 3 to 4 inches of entry clearance. Undersized clearance forces operators to tilt the mast, scuffing the pallet and risking load instability.

Deck style interacts with entry style. Open decks shed debris and reduce weight, which benefits cold-chain and wash-down environments, while solid or closed decks support small-footprint and bagged products that would otherwise sag between slats. Reversible pallets with identical top and bottom decks eliminate the orientation step entirely, speeding automated depalletizing. Buyers sourcing across a broad product mix should review the full Elipacko industrial packaging line to match deck patterns to specific SKUs rather than forcing one design onto every load type.

Integration With Automation and Racking

If your facility runs automated storage and retrieval systems, entry style and bottom-deck geometry must match the machine interface precisely. Shuttle and crane systems often require specific runner spacing and lead-in chamfers, and a four-way pallet with the wrong runner layout will jam a conveyor just as surely as a two-way pallet in a four-way application. Request CAD drawings and conveyability data before committing to a standard.

Racking introduces a separate consideration: unsupported rack-beam storage demands a pallet rated for racking load across a given span, and entry style influences how the bottom runners seat on the beams. Confirm the dynamic, static, and racking ratings independently, since a pallet may handle a heavy static load on the floor yet deflect unsafely when stored on beams.

Specification Checklist

  • Entry style: two-way, partial four-way, or full four-way matched to handling direction
  • Jack accessibility on the faces your ground-level pickers use most
  • Entry height clearance exceeding fork-tine thickness by a safe margin
  • Deck style aligned to product footprint and wash-down needs
  • Runner geometry verified against conveyor and ASRS interfaces
  • Racking rating confirmed separately from static and dynamic ratings

Making the Call

Standardizing fork entry style is a one-time decision with multi-year consequences. Map your dominant handling directions, automation interfaces, and racking spans, then specify the entry style that removes the most repositioning steps from your busiest workflows. A direct manufacturer relationship lets you validate these specs against real drawings and request custom runner layouts before the first production run, protecting throughput and avoiding costly fleet mismatches down the line.
